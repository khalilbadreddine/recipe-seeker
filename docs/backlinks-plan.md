## 0. Ground rules before you start

- **Minimum viable site before outreach:** all 24 recipes + 12 hubs live, no broken images, About page with the AI-author persona (name, story, photo), medical disclaimer present. Nobody links to a half-built site.
- **One spreadsheet tracks everything:** columns = target URL, site name, contact email/name, tactic, date contacted, follow-up date, status, link acquired (y/n). Create `docs/outreach-tracker.csv` — update it every session. This is the whole CRM.
- **Anchor text:** never repeat the same anchor. Rotate: brand ("The Recipe Seeker"), URL, generic ("this guide"), long-tail ("iron-rich meal ideas"). ~60% brand/URL/generic, ~40% descriptive.
- **Pace:** 5–10 outreach emails/week + journalist replies. Steady > bursts. A brand-new domain getting 50 links in 48h looks bought.

## 1. Digital PR angles (original data = your unfair advantage)

Journalists and bloggers link to **numbers they can cite**, not to "a nice recipe site." Your data asset: per-serving nutrient values for 24 recipes + USDA-sourced food tables on 12 hubs. Turn that into 3 linkable studies. Publish each as a short on-site article (e.g. `/data/protein-per-dollar/`), with one chart/table and a methodology note. Then pitch it (see §2).

**Study A — "Protein per dollar: 24 high-protein recipes ranked"**
- Method: cost each recipe's ingredients using Walmart/Kroger online prices (one afternoon of work), compute protein-per-dollar and protein-per-100-kcal.
- Headline stat example: "Lentils deliver ~X g protein per $1 — 4× cheaper than chicken breast."
- Pitch to: personal-finance + food journalists ("eating healthy on a budget"), fitness media.
- Asset to point at: the study page + `/guides/high-protein-meals-on-a-budget/`.

**Study B — "The iron gap: what 24 popular-style dinners actually deliver"**
- Method: tabulate iron per serving across your recipes vs. the 18 mg daily value; highlight which common dinner patterns fall short and which 5 meals close the gap fastest.
- Angle: iron deficiency is one of the most common nutrient deficiencies worldwide, especially in women 25–44 — your core demographic.
- Pitch to: women's health media, parenting blogs, dietitian bloggers.
- Asset: study page + `/guides/what-to-eat-for-iron-deficiency/` + `/nutrients/iron/`.

**Study C — "Vitamin D in winter: how much do popular dinners actually provide?"**
- Seasonal (pitch Oct–Jan). Method: vitamin D per serving across recipes vs. 15 mcg daily value; the honest finding will be "food alone barely dents it" — that honesty IS the story and earns dietitian links.
- Asset: `/nutrients/vitamin-d/`.

**How to run a study in one weekend:**
1. Saturday AM: pull the numbers from your own `recipes.json` (you already have per-serving values).
2. Saturday PM: write 600–900 words — headline stat up top, one table, one chart (screenshot of a Google Sheet is fine), methodology paragraph, disclaimer.
3. Sunday: publish, then send 15–20 pitches (§2 templates).

## 2. Journalist outreach (HARO/Connectively/Featured/Terkel)

**Setup (30 min, once):**

- Set alerts for: nutrition, recipes, healthy eating, iron deficiency, high protein, budget meals, meal prep.
- Respond **2–3× per week**, within hours of the query landing. Speed beats polish.

**Positioning:** you're "the founder of The Recipe Seeker, a nutrition-first recipe site" + the AI author persona (give her a name/title like "recipe developer focused on nutrient-dense cooking"). If/when you add a credentialed reviewer, lead with them. Never claim to be a dietitian if you're not.

## 3. Recipe roundup outreach

Food bloggers publish "25 High-Protein Dinners" roundups constantly. Each roundup links out to 20–30 recipes. Getting 2–3 of your recipes into each = steady contextual links.

**Find targets — exact Google queries (run monthly, take the top 20 results each):**
- `"high protein" roundup recipes 2026`
- `intitle:roundup "high-protein dinners"`
- `"iron rich" recipes roundup`
- `site:*.com "recipe roundup" "meal prep" high protein` (adapt per nutrient)
- `"15" OR "20" OR "25" "healthy" recipes roundup`
- Also: search Pinterest for roundup-style pins in your niche, click through to the blogs.

**Qualify (2 min per site):** real blog with traffic (check ~5 recent posts with dates), links out to other blogs' recipes (not just their own), contact page or email visible. Skip: link farms, sites with zero outbound links, anything that charges for inclusion without disclosure.

**Pitch template:**

> Subject: A [nutrient] recipe for your [roundup title] roundup
>
> Hi [name],
>
> Loved your roundup "[exact post title]" — [one genuine specific compliment, e.g. "the 15-minute filter is exactly how I cook on weeknights"].
>
> I run The Recipe Seeker, where every recipe is built around a nutrient target. Thought this might fit the list: [Recipe Name] — [one-line hook with the number, e.g. "38 g protein and 2.5 g omega-3s per serving, 30 minutes, one pan"]: [URL]
>
> Happy to write the 2–3 sentence blurb + provide the photo if you want to add it. Either way, great list.
>
> [Name]

**Cadence:** 5 pitches/week. Expect ~10–20% hit rate. When a recipe gets included, pin that roundup and thank them publicly (relationship > one link).

## 4. Resource-page link building (for the 12 nutrient hubs)

Universities, hospitals, dietitian practices, and nonprofits maintain "helpful resources" pages that link out by design. Your nutrient hubs (deficiency signs + food tables + recipes, with medical disclaimer) are exactly what belongs there.

**Find targets — exact queries (per nutrient, e.g. iron):**
- `"iron deficiency" inurl:resources`
- `"healthy eating" inurl:resources site:.edu`
- `site:.edu "nutrition resources" links`
- `"anemia diet" "useful links" OR "helpful resources"`
- `"vitamin d" resources inurl:links`
- `"high protein" "resources" dietitian`
- `intitle:resources "eating healthy on a budget"`

**Qualify:** page actually lists external links (not a link farm), `.edu`/`.org`/hospital/dietitian-practice domains preferred, check the page isn't dead (dates, working links).

**Pitch template:**

> Subject: Resource suggestion for your [page title] page
>
> Hi [name],
>
> Found your resources page at [URL] while researching [topic] — great list, especially [one specific linked resource].
>
> I run The Recipe Seeker, a nutrition-first recipe site. We have a free guide on [nutrient] — what it does, daily needs, top food sources with amounts, and recipes with per-serving nutrition from USDA data: [hub URL, e.g. therecipeseeker.com/nutrients/iron/]
>
> Might be a useful addition under your [section name]. No worries either way — keep up the good work.
>
> [Name]

**Cadence:** 5/week, rotate nutrients so you're not pitching the same hub twice to overlapping lists. `.edu` and hospital pages convert slowly (committees, webmasters) — follow up once after 3 weeks, then move on.

## 5. Food blogger collaboration (safe link swaps)

**The safe pattern — "recipe feature swap," not reciprocal linking schemes:**
- You feature their recipe in one of your roundup-style posts ("10 Dietitian-Approved High-Iron Dinners" — linking out to 10 bloggers), email each one: "featured you here." 30–50% will share it; many link back from a future roundup or resources page. This is how food-blog linking actually works — editorial, one-directional at a time, relationship-driven.
- **Expert roundup post:** "9 Dietitians on the Easiest High-Protein Breakfast" — email 15–20 RDs/food bloggers with one question, publish answers with their photo + link. Contributors share + link to "their feature." Template:

> Subject: Quick quote for a dietitian roundup? (2 min)
>
> Hi [name],
>
> I'm putting together "9 Dietitians on the Easiest High-Protein Breakfast" for The Recipe Seeker (nutrition-first recipes, [X] monthly readers).
>
> One question: what's the single easiest high-protein breakfast you recommend to clients, and why? 2–3 sentences is perfect. I'll feature your photo, bio, and a link to [their site].
>
> Deadline: [date, ~10 days out]. Happy to return the favor with a feature anytime.
>
> [Name]

**What's safe vs. not:**
- ✅ SAFE: featuring others and telling them; contributor roundups; genuine guest posts (see below); natural reciprocal links that happen organically over time between real sites.
- ❌ NOT SAFE: "I'll link you if you link me" bulk exchanges, three-way link schemes, sidebar/footer/blogroll swaps with 30 sites, private blog networks. Google explicitly calls excessive reciprocal linking a link scheme.

**Guest posting (only if the blog is real):** pitch a specific article, not "can I write for you." One contextual in-content link to a relevant hub/recipe. 2–4 guest posts in 90 days is plenty; quality > quantity. Skip any site that charges a "publishing fee" without marking it sponsored — that's a paid link, disclose or decline.

## 6. Broken-link building

**Process (1 hour/week):**
1. Take 10 resource pages from §4's prospect list.
2. Run the free **Check My Links** Chrome extension on each. Note every broken outbound link (404s) — especially ones pointing at dead nutrition guides or retired food blogs.
3. Check what the dead page was via the Wayback Machine (1 min) — confirm your hub/recipe is a genuine replacement for the same topic.
4. Email:

> Subject: Broken link on your [page title] page (+ replacement)
>
> Hi [name],
>
> Quick heads-up: on [their page URL], the link "[anchor text]" under [section] goes to a 404 — looks like [dead site] took the page down.
>
> We have a free [topic] guide that covers the same ground (food sources with amounts + recipes with per-serving USDA nutrition): [your URL]. Could work as a replacement if helpful.
>
> Either way, figured you'd want to know about the broken link.
>
> [Name]

Hit rate is low (~5%) but each link is high-quality and the email does them a favor, so it never burns the relationship.

## 8. What NOT to do (and why)

1. **Buying bulk link packages** (Fiverr, "100 DA50 backlinks $20") — links from penalized/spam networks Google already discounts or flags; cleanup takes months. The #1 way new sites get burned.
2. **Link farms / PBNs** — networks of fake blogs linking to each other. Google's spam systems target these specifically; one manual action can zero your traffic for a year.
3. **Spammy directories** — "free backlink lists," auto-submit tools. Worthless at best (ignored), harmful at worst (toxic neighborhood). Only exception: curated, human-reviewed, niche-relevant directories (e.g. a real dietitian directory) — treat as citations, not strategy.
4. **Link bursts** — 50 links in 48 hours on a 2-month-old domain screams manipulation. Steady 2–4/week equivalent.
5. **Exact-match anchor repetition** — 20 links all saying "high iron recipes" = over-optimization footprint. Vary anchors (§0).
6. **Paid links without `rel="sponsored"`** — violates Google's guidelines; if you ever pay for placement, demand the sponsored attribute.
7. **Reciprocal-link schemes** — "link to me and I'll link to you" at scale, three-way swaps, blogroll rings. A few natural reciprocal links between real sites are fine; organized exchange is a scheme.
8. **Comment/forum/profile spam** — auto-commenting "great post! [link]" on blogs, forums, profiles. Nofollowed, ignored, and brand-poisoning.
9. **Ignoring nofollow** — Pinterest, Reddit, Quora links are nofollow but drive traffic, brand searches, and AI-answer pickup. A natural profile *has* nofollows; a dofollow-only profile looks bought.
10. **Outreach spam** — mail-merge blasts with no personalization, no follow-up discipline, pitching irrelevant sites. Burns your domain reputation (email deliverability) and your brand. 5 genuinely personalized emails beat 100 blasts.

## Appendix: before-you-send checklist (every email)

- [ ] Read their last 2 posts; include one specific compliment/reference
- [ ] Link points at the most relevant page (hub > homepage for resource pages; specific recipe for roundups)
- [ ] Subject line under 50 chars, no clickbait
- [ ] One clear, low-effort ask (add a link / answer one question)
- [ ] Logged in `docs/outreach-tracker.csv` with follow-up date (+5–7 days, max 1 follow-up; 3 weeks for .edu)
- [ ] From a real name + real email (not noreply@), plain text, no attachments
